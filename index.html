Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Mac Dang, Athena Lin, Gian Cortez, Christopher Nguyen — mbdang@ucsd.edu, atl012@ucsd.edu, gicortez@ucsd.edu, chn058@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Naming more officers in a complaint itself increases the likelihood that at least one allegation will be substantiated.

An allegation is substantiated when the Civilian Complaint Review Board finds sufficient evidence to support a finding of officer misconduct. We compare two interpretations of why complaints naming more officers have higher substantiation rates.

Both figures use the NYC CCRB allegations dataset supplied for this assignment: 33,358 allegation records covering 12,056 complaints and 3,996 officers. The dataset includes only officers with at least one substantiated allegation, so these findings describe this selected dataset rather than all NYPD officers or complaints.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

Score: −2 is fully deceptive; +2 is fully earnest.

  • Decision 1: Counting a complaint as a “win” if any allegation was substantiated (Score: −1.5)
    We used the percentage of complaints with at least one substantiated allegation rather than the percentage of individual allegation records substantiated. Complaints naming more officers also contain more allegation records: about 9 on average for complaints naming four or more officers, compared with about 2 for complaints naming one officer. This gives larger complaints more opportunities to count as a “win.” We chose the complaint-level measure because it directly answers whether a complaint had any substantiated allegation. The deceptive part is using that valid measure to suggest that naming more officers itself explains the difference, without emphasizing the different numbers of allegations.
  • Decision 2: Grouping 4–8 officers into one “4+” bar (Score: −1)
    The “4+ officers” bar combines complaints naming anywhere from four to eight officers. It is also the smallest group, with just 93 complaints, but we displayed it like the larger groups without showing sample sizes or uncertainty. Combining these complaints makes the comparison easier to read, while hiding differences within the group. Showing four, five, and six or more officers separately would reveal more detail, but the small groups would make the pattern less stable and visually less simple.
  • Decision 3: Adding a “3x” arrow over the bars (Score: +1)
    The “3x” annotation summarizes an approximately accurate comparison: 78% divided by 26% is three. It gives readers an easy number to remember without requiring them to calculate the ratio. The large arrow also makes the difference feel more dramatic and directs attention to the endpoints rather than the middle groups. A version without the arrow would be quieter, but would make the main comparison less immediate. We scored this positively because it highlights a supported numerical comparison, while recognizing that its size adds persuasive emphasis.
  • Decision 4: Using a light-to-dark green color scale (Score: +2)
    We made the bars darker as officer count increased. The ordered colors reinforce the ordered categories and help readers follow the comparison from one officer to four or more. Using one color for every bar would also be accurate, but would provide less visual guidance. We consider this earnest because the color order follows the officer-count order and does not represent an additional outcome or claim statistical certainty.
  • Decision 5: Using a title that suggests causation (Score: −1.5)
    The phrase “Name four officers” sounds like an action someone could take to improve the result of a complaint. Our data compares existing complaints; it does not show what would happen if someone added another officer to the same complaint. The title also simplifies the four-or-more group to “four.” A title such as “Complaints naming more officers have higher substantiation rates” would describe the association more carefully, but would be less persuasive about our causal proposition.

AGAINST the Proposition

Counting note: an allegation record is one row in the dataset. A distinct allegation type counts each different allegation label once within a complaint, even if that label appears in multiple records. This explains why the supporting rationale reports about 9 records for complaints naming four or more officers, while the comparison above reports 4.4 distinct allegation types.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

Score: −2 is fully deceptive; +2 is fully earnest.

  • Decision 1: Using allegation-type count instead of officer count on the x-axis (Score: +1)
    We switched the x-axis to the number of distinct allegation types and showed another rising pattern, from 18% to 69%. This gives readers a possible explanation for the officer-count pattern: complaints naming more officers also tend to contain more allegation types. We consider it useful and mostly earnest to show this additional relationship. However, it does not establish that officer count has no effect. Comparing officer counts within groups with the same allegation-type count would address that question more directly.
  • Decision 2: Framing allegation-type count as incident breadth (Score: −1)
    Our annotation calls the measure “incident breadth,” encouraging readers to interpret more allegation types as a bigger or more complex incident. However, the number of allegation types does not directly measure incident severity, witnesses, or available evidence. This makes the complexity explanation sound more established than our analysis can show. Calling it “number of distinct allegation types” throughout would be more precise, but would provide a less intuitive story about why the complaints differ.
  • Decision 3: Bucketing six or more allegation types into one group (Score: −1)
    We combined all complaints with six or more allegation types into a single point. This keeps the end of the chart simple and makes the overall rise easier to follow, but it hides differences among complaints within that group. Showing every count separately would reveal more variation, while also creating smaller groups and a less simple trend. Displaying the sample size helps readers judge the pooled group, but does not reveal all of its internal differences.
  • Decision 4: Shading the area under the line (Score: +1)
    We used light shading to help readers follow the rising pattern across allegation-type groups. The vertical axis begins at zero, and the labeled points show the actual rates. A plain line would communicate the same values with less visual weight, but the shading makes the main pattern easier to notice. We scored this positively because it supports the displayed trend, while recognizing that a smooth filled shape can make the relationship feel more consistent than the grouped data alone establishes.

Final Reflection

Building the visualizations was the straightforward part; the difficult part was deciding which interpretation the evidence supported. The supporting chart makes naming more officers look like the reason complaints are substantiated more often. However, complaints naming four or more officers also contain more distinct allegation types: an average of 4.4, compared with 2.0 for complaints naming one officer. Complaints with six or more allegation types had at least one substantiated allegation 69% of the time, compared with 18% for complaints with one type. We lean toward the opposing interpretation because complaint breadth offers another possible explanation. These patterns do not establish what causes the difference or whether officer count has an effect of its own.

We define ethical analysis and visualization as making sure the impression a chart creates matches what the data supports. This requires explaining what was counted, how groups were formed, and which comparisons were left out. Persuasive choices such as color and annotations can help readers notice a meaningful pattern. They become misleading when they hide information that could change the reader’s conclusion, such as small sample sizes, the difference between complaint-level and allegation-level rates, or the use of allegation-type count as a measure of incident complexity. For us, the boundary is whether readers receive enough context to judge the argument for themselves.

What surprised us was how persuasive both views could become through choices such as pooling the last group, highlighting a ratio, and framing allegation count as incident breadth. We did not need to change the numbers to encourage different conclusions. This made us realize that accurate numbers alone do not make a visualization ethical: the measures, comparisons, labels, and missing context also matter. We found that acknowledging the limits of both arguments was more defensible than treating either visualization as proof of causation.
